How to Raise Your SAT Math Score by 100+ Points

6 min read

A 100+ point jump on SAT Math sounds dramatic, but it's usually not the result of learning dramatically more math. It's the result of fixing a small number of specific, fixable problems — most students are leaving points on the table in the same handful of places.

1. Know exactly where your points are going missing

"I'm bad at math" is not specific enough to fix. Take a full-length timed practice test and actually categorize every miss: careless arithmetic error, misread the question, didn't know the concept, or ran out of time. These four categories need completely different fixes — and most students, without tracking this, assume it's mostly "didn't know the concept" when it's often mostly the other three.

2. Fix the no-calculator section first

The no-calculator questions are where relative scores separate the most, because they reward fluency, not just knowledge. If you're doing long division or multi-step algebra by hand slowly, that's minutes stolen from questions you could otherwise get right. Speed here comes from repetition, not new knowledge — drill the same basic operations until they're automatic.

3. Learn to recognize question types, not just solve them

The SAT reuses a limited set of question structures — systems of equations disguised as word problems, quadratics that need factoring versus the quadratic formula, function transformations. Once you can pattern-match "this is a linear systems problem" in the first five seconds, you save time that compounds across the whole section.

4. Grid-in questions are where points hide

Multiple choice questions let you eliminate answers and guess intelligently. Grid-in questions don't — you either know it or you don't, and there's no partial credit for "close." Many students under-practice grid-ins specifically because they feel harder, which is exactly why they're worth extra deliberate practice.

5. Timed practice beats untimed practice, every time

Working through problems calmly, with unlimited time, teaches you to solve problems. It does not teach you to solve problems in 75 seconds with 21 other questions waiting. The transfer from "I can solve this" to "I can solve this on test day" only happens under realistic time pressure — which is why full-length, properly timed practice tests matter more than extra untimed drilling once you know the fundamentals.
